# Fatigue, movement and food in SLE with coeliac disease

A report for a 26 year old woman living with systemic lupus erythematosus and
coeliac disease. September 2026.

---

## First, the part that matters most

This report is an orientation. It is not a review article, not a second opinion
and not a treatment plan. It is written so that you can walk into a clinic with
it and ask better questions.

**The original papers could not be opened while this was written.** The network
this was produced on does not reach medical databases. They could be searched
for, not read. That has one consequence, and it belongs here at the top rather
than in small print at the bottom:

> Individual figures from studies, meaning percentages, effect sizes and study
> populations, appear almost nowhere in this report, on purpose. What you get
> instead is the direction of the evidence and how strong it is.

In exchange, every section says how solid it is, and the source documents are
listed at the end with their identifiers. A PMID or a DOI finds the paper in
seconds, and any doctor has access. This report is built as a signpost to the
sources, not as a substitute for them.

Where something is not established, it says so. That is not politeness. A great
deal circulates about lupus, and the difference between a guideline, a small
trial and a plausible guess is exactly the difference that matters.

### What this report cannot do

It does not know your blood results, which organs are involved, or your history.
Nearly every decision in lupus hangs on those. It says nothing about any dose,
not because that is secret but because a dose without the person in front of you
is meaningless.

And it is a snapshot, not a subscription. Lupus care has moved a lot in recent
years. As this text ages it does not become wrong, it becomes incomplete.

## Part 1: The fatigue

### Why it does not track your blood results

This is where most consultations go wrong. The results look fine, the
inflammation is quiet, and there is still not enough in the tank for an ordinary
day.

That is not a contradiction and it is not imagination. It is the best described
finding in the whole subject: fatigue in lupus runs largely independently of
measured disease activity. What it does track closely, in study after study, is
sleep quality, pain, deconditioning, low mood, and a coexisting fibromyalgia.

Two things follow. First, a good lab report does not disprove the exhaustion.
Second, and this is the more hopeful half: the things it does track are partly
things you can work on. That is a better place to push than waiting for a number
to improve.

In patient surveys, fatigue consistently ranks among the most burdensome parts
of the illness, often ahead of pain and ahead of the skin. It is still discussed
less often in clinic than the numbers are.

*Strength of evidence: strong for the decoupling from disease activity. Strong
for the association with sleep, pain, mood and fibromyalgia.*

### What to rule out before blaming the lupus

This is the most practically useful section in the report, because everything in
it is treatable and routinely missed. With coeliac disease in the picture the
list is longer than usual, because absorption through the gut may be impaired.

- **Anaemia and iron deficiency.** Common, more common again in coeliac disease,
  and fatigue is the main symptom. Ferritin belongs in the request, not just a
  full blood count.
- **Thyroid.** Autoimmune conditions cluster. An underactive thyroid looks from
  the outside exactly like lupus fatigue.
- **Vitamin B12, folate, vitamin D.** The classic gaps in coeliac disease.
- **Kidney function and urine.** The kidney does not announce itself.
- **Sleep apnoea and sleep quality.** Nobody who does not recover at night is
  rested during the day, and that has nothing to do with lupus.
- **Depression and anxiety.** Both are common in chronic illness, both cause
  fatigue, both are treatable. Checking is diligence, not an accusation.
- **Medication.** Some of it is sedating. Worth going through once.
- **Fibromyalgia.** It frequently accompanies lupus and is treated differently
  from the lupus itself. Recognising it changes the plan.

*Strength: clinical practice and guideline logic, not a single trial.*

### What actually helps

Honestly, up front: the evidence here is thin and it disagrees with itself.

A Cochrane review in 2023 on physical exercise in lupus reached very cautious
conclusions. The included trials were small, mostly unblinded, and the certainty
of the evidence was rated low. An earlier synthesis from 2017, reading largely
the same small set of trials, did find improvements in aerobic capacity and in
fatigue, as did older controlled trials. Note the direction: the most recent
review is the most cautious one, not the other way round.

That disagreement is not smoothed over here. It looks like this:

- For **physical capacity**, the effect of training is clearest.
- For **fatigue itself**, it is less clear, but reported repeatedly.
- On **safety**, the trials are consistent: exercise did not worsen disease
  activity. With the important caveat in Part 2: that applies to stable disease.

EULAR published dedicated recommendations on fatigue in inflammatory rheumatic
disease in 2024. They put physical activity and psychologically grounded
approaches at the front, and state that managing fatigue is a job for the whole
team, not the rheumatologist alone.

What is deliberately **not** in this report is a supplement with evidence behind
it for lupus fatigue. There is none worth the name.

*Strength: low to moderate. Conflicting syntheses, small and mostly unblinded
trials. Clearer for aerobic capacity than for fatigue.*

## Part 2: Exercise, including during a flare

### The honest position

The question was whether to exercise during an active flare. Precision matters
here more than a snappy answer.

**There is no trial on this.** Essentially every exercise study in lupus enrolled
people with quiet or low disease activity and explicitly excluded active disease.
The reassuring line "exercise does not worsen lupus" is a statement about stable
periods. It does not transfer to an acute flare.

### What does exist

An international task force consensus from 2024. In substance it says:

- During a flare, **caution** applies, and whether anything currently argues
  against exertion should be reassessed.
- In a flare with inflamed joints, **those joints specifically should not be
  loaded**.
- Those with quiet or mild disease should follow the general physical activity
  recommendations, the same ones that apply to everyone.

Translated into daily life, that does not mean bed and it does not mean pushing
through. It means getting smaller rather than stopping. Walking instead of
intervals. Stretching and gentle movement instead of strength work. Leaving
inflamed joints out. And a new flare should be reported before the programme is
adjusted, not after.

### The exclusions that get forgotten

The same recommendation states that with cardiac, pulmonary or renal
involvement, on anticoagulation, or with osteonecrosis, exertion should be
assessed medically **before** it is increased. That is not small print. It is the
reason no training prescription appears in this report.

*Strength: for the flare, expert consensus only, no trials. For stable disease,
moderate.*

### How to start when stairs are already hard

With an amount that feels too small, and that you can still manage on a bad day.
That is not modesty, it is the method. The commonest reason movement fails in
fatigue is a good day on which too much gets done, followed by three days in bed.

In the trials that showed something, programmes typically ran eight to twelve
weeks, at moderate intensity, and were supervised. Supervised means somebody was
looking. In the analyses that was one of the differences between programmes that
worked and programmes that did not.

A workable start: a fixed, small daily amount, the same on good days and bad
ones, and only after one or two weeks without payback, a little more.

### Pacing

Pacing means spreading your energy across the day and resting on purpose
**before** you run out, rather than continuing until you crash.

Honestly: for lupus there is very little research on it, only small education
programmes and an ongoing pilot. The better evidence on pacing comes from other
conditions, mainly ME/CFS and long covid, and it is mixed there too. Whether
lupus fatigue behaves like that fatigue is not settled.

It is here anyway because it is cheap, harmless and reversible. Trying it for two
weeks and looking at your own record costs nothing.

*Strength: weak in lupus. Extrapolated from other conditions, where results are
mixed.*

## Part 3: Eating with lupus

### Is there a lupus diet?

No. There is no way of eating that treats lupus.

What exists are signals, mostly from observational studies, that a Mediterranean
pattern goes along with lower disease activity and with better cardiovascular
markers. Observational means people who eat that way tend to do better. Whether
the food is the cause is not something those studies show.

The second reason it is in this report is better established and often
overlooked: people with lupus carry a markedly raised cardiovascular risk, and
earlier in life than others. The evidence for a Mediterranean pattern on the
heart and vessels is good, even though it comes from the general population.
That alone is reason enough.

In practice a Mediterranean pattern means plenty of vegetables, pulses, fruit,
olive oil and fish, little red and processed meat, little sugar. With coeliac
disease wheat drops out as the grain base. The rest stays, and the rest is most
of it.

*Strength: weak and observational for lupus activity. Good for heart and
vessels, but extrapolated from the general population.*

### Supplements

- **Vitamin D.** Frequently low in lupus, and a deficiency should be corrected.
  That is not disputed. What is not established is that dosing beyond correction
  improves fatigue. With coeliac disease and on steroids, vitamin D also matters
  for bone, see Part 5.
- **Omega-3.** Small trials, some showing improvement in inflammatory markers and
  wellbeing. Not enough for a recommendation, little harm expected. On
  anticoagulation it should be discussed.
- **Turmeric, green tea extract and similar.** Weak evidence. High dose extracts
  are a different thing from a spice, and there are reports of liver injury with
  turmeric extracts. This belongs in the consultation before you start.
- **Iron.** Only after measurement, never on suspicion.

One rule covers all of it: in lupus, treat every supplement as a medicine until
shown otherwise, because some of them stimulate the immune system and because
interactions exist.

*Strength: omega-3 weak to moderate. Vitamin D for fatigue, very weak. Other
preparations, weak.*

### What is better left alone

- **Alfalfa sprouts and alfalfa containing supplements.** The caution rests on
  the amino acid L-canavanine, on case reports and on animal work. That is not
  strong evidence, but alfalfa is dispensable, so leaving it out is reasonable.
- **Echinacea and similar immune stimulating preparations.** Same argument, from
  the logic of the disease: a substance meant to stimulate the immune system is
  not a good idea in an autoimmune one. The data are thin here too, and here too
  the cost of abstaining is nothing.
- **Smoking.** Not food, but it belongs on this list. The association with higher
  disease activity and with poorer response to hydroxychloroquine has been
  synthesised across multiple studies. It is the best evidenced behaviour change
  in this entire report.
- **Very high salt intake.** The rationale comes from laboratory work and from
  blood pressure. No lupus specific human evidence.
- **Alcohol.** Inconsistent findings in lupus itself. It becomes relevant through
  the liver when medication is involved. And beer is a gluten question anyway.

*Strength: weak. Case reports, animal work and plausibility. Defensible as
precaution, not as fact.*

### Weight and steroids

Once steroids are involved the subject shifts. Appetite, fluid retention and
redistribution of fat are effects of the drug, not a question of discipline. That
needs saying, because the opposite belief is widespread and leads to diets that
achieve nothing in this situation.

What counts in that phase is simpler: enough protein, enough calcium and vitamin
D for bone, salt on the low side, and movement, because it works against the
muscle loss steroids cause.

## Part 4: Coeliac disease

### The rule without exceptions

In coeliac disease the gluten free diet is not one diet among several, it is the
treatment. It applies for life, and it applies even when a small amount causes
nothing you can feel. Damage to the intestinal lining also happens without
symptoms.

Out: wheat in every form, which includes spelt, emmer, einkorn, khorasan and
green spelt, plus barley and rye, malt and malt extract, barley beer, and wheat
starch without the corresponding labelling.

*Strength: high for the treatment itself.*

### How strict is strict?

The threshold used as an orientation in guidelines and trials is very low, in the
range of a few milligrams of gluten per day. The exact figure is not uniform
across professional bodies, and the studies behind it are small.

The practical consequence does not depend on the exact number: this is about
traces, not portions. That is why labelling and cross contamination are the real
subject, and not giving up bread.

*Strength: high for the treatment, weak to moderate for the precise threshold.*

### What actually counts in a kitchen

Here everyday anxiety is often distributed differently from the measurements.
This is the most useful section of this chapter.

**Genuinely relevant:**

- **Shared fryers.** Wheat batter in the same oil leaves measurable gluten in
  gluten free food fried in it. Eating out, this is the commonest invisible
  source.
- **Shared pasta water.** Same principle.
- **Flour dust in the air.** When baking it settles, including on things standing
  finished nearby.
- **Unlabelled ingredients.** Sauce thickeners, malt flavouring, spice blends,
  some medicines and supplements.
- **Eating out.** By a distance the commonest source. Not because of the kitchen
  as such, but because you cannot see what happens in it.

**Overrated, if cleaned first:**

- Shared toasters, when a separate bag or tray is used
- Shared cutlery and crockery out of the dishwasher
- Shared worktops after wiping down
- Shared chopping boards of smooth material after washing

This is not meant as reassurance but as a redistribution of attention. Anyone who
aims their worry at toasters and cutlery, and then does not ask about the fryer
in a restaurant, is guarding the wrong door.

*Strength: weak to moderate. Small, unblinded measurement studies, but the only
numbers there are.*

### Oats

Pure, uncontaminated oats are tolerated by most people with coeliac disease. This
has been examined in syntheses of several trials, in which the certainty of the
evidence was low. A small proportion react anyway.

In practice: only oats explicitly labelled gluten free, introduced slowly, and if
symptoms appear, dropped again and discussed. Ordinary supermarket oats are
almost always contaminated with wheat.

*Strength: moderate for the safety of pure oats, with low certainty in the
synthesis.*

### Why the tTG result is not enough

This is the section that surprises people most often.

A normal tTG IgA on a gluten free diet is often read as meaning everything is
fine. A synthesis of several studies showed that the antibodies fail to flag
persistent villous atrophy in a large share of cases. The order of magnitude
given there is around half of those with ongoing damage.

What follows: the antibodies are a marker of trend, not proof of a healed lining.
If symptoms or fatigue persist on a strict gluten free diet, a normal tTG is not
a reason to stop looking.

*Strength: moderate to high for the limited informativeness of serology.*

### The traps of a gluten free kitchen

Gluten free is not automatically good. Commercial gluten free baked goods are
often lower in fibre, higher in fat and higher in sugar than their counterparts,
and frequently not fortified.

What runs short as a result: fibre, iron, folate, B12, calcium, zinc and
magnesium.

What fixes it: rice, maize, buckwheat, millet, quinoa, amaranth, potatoes,
pulses, nuts and seeds as the base instead of the packaged goods. Which is
essentially what Part 3 suggests for the lupus. That is the good news of this
report: the two sets of rules pull in the same direction.

## Part 5: Where lupus and coeliac disease get in each other's way

### Are they connected?

Yes, in the sense of clustering. Autoimmune conditions occur together more often
than chance would predict, and this is described for lupus and coeliac disease.

How strongly is open. Estimates in the literature diverge considerably depending
on who was studied and how. So no figure appears here. The association is
established, the magnitude is not.

### The nutrients that fail twice

This is the real interface, and it matters more in practice than any question of
cause.

| What | Why it runs short in coeliac disease | Why it counts in lupus |
|---|---|---|
| Iron | absorption in the upper small bowel impaired | fatigue, often the actual reason |
| Vitamin D | absorption impaired, gluten free products rarely fortified | bone, especially on steroids |
| Calcium | the same | bone, especially on steroids |
| B12 and folate | absorption impaired, gluten free flour not fortified | fatigue, blood count |
| Zinc | absorption impaired | wound healing, immune function |

The consequence for daily life is simple: these belong measured, not estimated.
That is why they are on the question list for the appointment.

### Bone, at 26

At 26 nobody thinks about bone density. But two things meet here: impaired
absorption of calcium and vitamin D in coeliac disease, and steroids, if they are
or were in the picture.

Steroids have their own guidelines, because bone loss begins early, often within
the first six months. They set out a risk assessment, adequate calcium and
vitamin D, and depending on risk a bone density scan.

Being young is not an argument against. It only changes the arithmetic, because
many decades follow.

*Strength: guideline based.*

### If fatigue persists on a strict gluten free diet

Then the order runs roughly like this, and it belongs in a consultation rather
than in a private search:

1. Measure the nutrients: iron and ferritin, B12, folate, vitamin D, full blood
   count.
2. Check the thyroid.
3. Look for hidden gluten. Medicines and supplements count, not only food.
4. Remember that a normal tTG does not exclude persistent villous atrophy.
5. Consider other causes, such as lactose intolerance, small intestinal
   bacterial overgrowth, irritable bowel, sleep disorders, depression.
6. And only then is the fatigue a lupus question.

*Strength: clinical practice and guideline logic.*

## Part 6: What current treatment looks like

This part says nothing about doses and replaces no conversation. It is here so
you know what the treatment is oriented towards.

- **Hydroxychloroquine for practically everyone.** Current guidelines treat it as
  the foundation of treatment, not an add on.
- **Steroids as low and as briefly as possible.** Guidelines of recent years have
  become markedly stricter about this. The aim is a low maintenance dose or none,
  and other drugs are brought in earlier to make that possible.
- **A target rather than a feeling.** Treatment is oriented towards a defined
  state of low activity or remission, which is measured. Whether anything changes
  follows from that.
- **Regular blood and urine monitoring**, including when you feel well. Kidney
  involvement causes no symptoms early on.
- **Eye screening on hydroxychloroquine.** On a fixed schedule with visual fields
  and OCT. The point is early detection: if early damage is found and the drug
  stopped, it usually does not progress.
- **Sun protection.** UV can trigger skin and systemic flares. There is a
  controlled human trial behind this, which is rare in this field.

*Strength: guidelines, the highest level available.*

## Part 7: What belongs to this stage of life

- **Pregnancy, even if it is not current.** Pregnancies in lupus go better when
  planned during a quiet phase. Some drugs have to be switched beforehand, others,
  hydroxychloroquine among them, are explicitly continued. Worth discussing once
  before it becomes urgent.
- **Contraception.** With antiphospholipid antibodies, particular considerations
  apply to oestrogen containing contraception. Whether those antibodies are
  present is a question you want answered.
- **Vaccination.** On immunosuppression live vaccines are an issue, and the rest
  of the schedule matters more than usual. Best reviewed before starting
  treatment.
- **Sun and smoking.** As above. Both are under your own control, and both are
  evidenced.

## Part 8: Measuring yourself, and what can be learned from it

The **Anker** app that accompanies this report is a diary. It makes no diagnosis
and calculates nothing a doctor should be calculating. It does two things.

**First, it changes the conversation at an appointment.** The difference between
"I often feel bad" and "on 21 of 30 days fatigue was above 6, and it has been
higher for three weeks" is large. The second can be acted on.

**Second, it can answer individual questions about you alone.** This is the idea
of a trial with a single participant. It works like this:

1. Change one thing, not three.
2. Hold it long enough, two to four weeks.
3. Write down beforehand how you would recognise that it helped.
4. Then look at the record.
5. If it stays unclear, switch back and watch again.

Questions that can be answered this way: does fatigue rise the day after
exercise? Does a fixed bedtime change anything? Does pacing produce fewer bad
days?

Questions that **cannot**: anything where the course of the disease itself plays
a part. A flare comes and goes on its own, and then whatever happened to start
just before it looks effective.

## Part 9: Do and do not, briefly

**Do**

- Take hydroxychloroquine, including when nothing hurts.
- Sun protection, daily, all year.
- Movement in a dose that also works on a bad day.
- Gluten free without exceptions, watching the fryer, the pasta water and the
  labelling rather than the toaster.
- Have iron, ferritin, vitamin D, B12, folate and calcium measured.
- Have urine checked, including without symptoms.
- Keep the eye screening in the calendar.
- Record what you will later want to compare.

**Do not**

- Smoke.
- Take alfalfa or immune stimulating preparations.
- Start any new supplement without asking, including herbal ones.
- Load inflamed joints during a flare.
- Increase training after a good day.
- Conclude from a normal tTG that the lining is fine.
- Attribute fatigue to the lupus before Part 1 has been worked through.

**Get seen immediately** (the list is in the app under Knowledge, Warning signs)

- New weakness, speech or vision disturbance, seizure
- Breathlessness, chest pain, coughing blood
- Fever on immunosuppression
- Frothy urine, rapidly increasing swelling, passing little urine
- A swollen, painful leg on one side

## Appendix A: Questions for the next appointment

This list is also in the app, where it can be ticked off.

1. How active is my lupus right now, as a number?
2. When was urine last checked for protein?
3. Which values should be checked how often, and who arranges it?
4. When is the next retinal screening due?
5. What is my steroid dose, and what is the plan for reducing it?
6. Have iron, ferritin, vitamin D, B12, folate and calcium been measured
   recently?
7. When was the last bone density scan, and is one needed?
8. How has my tTG moved over time?
9. Which vaccinations am I missing, and which may I not have on this treatment?
10. What do I do if I get a fever or an infection, and who do I call?
11. Which contraception suits my situation, and are antiphospholipid antibodies
    present?
12. May I exercise, and how much, including when I feel bad?
